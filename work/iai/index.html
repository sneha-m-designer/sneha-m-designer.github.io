01 · Overview

One product, two surfaces: a web app and a Figma plugin

Illustrations AI lets brands create illustrations in their own visual style. A team can either train the AI on 10–15 of its existing illustrations, or start from ready-made illustration packs. After that, anyone can describe what they need in a prompt and get an illustration that looks like it belongs to the brand.

I designed the web app end to end: onboarding, home, styles, packs, generation, library and credits. I then took the core job, prompt → on-brand illustration, into a Figma plugin, so designers could generate and insert illustrations without leaving their canvas.

02 · The problem

Custom illustrations are slow, costly and hard to keep on-brand

Teams get illustrations in one of two ways today. Both break down at scale.

  • ⏱️Slow by handA detailed illustration takes 10 to 70+ hours of an illustrator's time, plus review rounds.
  • 💸Expensive to scaleEvery new asset, and every revision, means more illustrator hours or agency cost.
  • 🎨Off-brand AI outputGeneric AI tools don't remember a brand's style, so results drift from image to image.
  • 🔀Tool switchingDesigners leave Figma, generate elsewhere, then import and clean up every asset.
Problem statement

Designers and brands spend significant time and money creating custom illustrations. Existing AI tools make them switch contexts, clean up assets by hand and regenerate repeatedly to get close to their brand style.

The opportunity

AI is already part of design work: 93% of designers in India use AI tools, according to a Figma survey. Yet there was no tool that generated illustrations in a brand's own style.

03 · Research

Two kinds of users, two different ways of asking

I reviewed how people used the early product and the prompts they typed, mapped who needs illustrations and why, and studied tools like Storyset, Blush, DrawKit and Canva. The clearest finding: people don't all know what they want to see.

User group 1

Intent-driven users

“I know what I need. Just draw it for me.”

They arrive with a clear picture and write precise prompts, like “Isometric office desk with laptop and coffee mug.”

  • Need: accuracy, style consistency, control over detail
  • Design for them: prompt tips, editing after generation, saved prompt formats
User group 2

Context-driven users

“Here's what I'm writing about. You tell me what to show.”

They paste the content they're working on, like “5 tips to boost your Instagram engagement,” and expect the AI to find the visual.

  • Need: help turning words into a visual idea, without design vocabulary
  • Design for them: ready-made packs, use-case presets, suggested prompts

Who uses illustrations, and where

  • Designers (brand, product, UI, visual): website and app UI, brand assets, feature explainers
  • Marketing and content teams: social posts, blog banners, campaigns, newsletters
  • Founders and product teams: landing pages, pitch decks, product updates

What I took from other tools

  • Storyset: a minimal home page lowers the load, like Google's search page
  • Canva: an upgrade pop-up right after login felt pushy, so I kept upsells contextual
  • Pricing: plans in this space are for individuals or teams, so onboarding asks which you are

04 · The web app

From first sign-in to an on-brand illustration

1

Onboarding that sets up the right path

Three quick questions: are you an individual, a team or just exploring, what describes your work, and what you'll use illustrations for. The answers tailor what people see first, and match how plans are sold.

2

A home page with two clear ways to start

One path for each user group: Generate with prompt for intent-driven users and Generate with pack for context-driven users. A Create style banner shows the product's core promise, and Recent shows every generation and training job with its status.

3

Create a style the AI can learn

Users name the style and upload 10–15 sample illustrations. A live style accuracy meter shows when more images will help, guidelines beside the upload explain what makes a good sample, and an honest note says training takes about 20 minutes.

4

Generate, size and export in one place

Pick a style, write a prompt and choose where the illustration will be used, with presets from 1:1 to 16:9 or a custom size. The preview, export formats (PNG, SVG, JPG, AI, EPS) and credit cost sit right next to it, and in-progress generations stay visible on the side.

5

Packs and a library for reuse

Themed packs give context-driven users a running start, and every generated illustration is saved to the library to reuse later, so the brand's illustration set keeps growing.

05 · The Figma plugin

Bringing the core job into Figma, in a 2-day sprint

Designers were leaving Figma to generate illustrations, then importing and cleaning them up. The plugin's goal was simple: prompt, preview and insert a brand-style illustration without leaving the canvas, and save it for reuse.

It had to be fast and need no onboarding, because a plugin gets opened mid-task. So I went through three iterations, cutting steps each time until it came down to two jobs: generate with a prompt or generate with a pack.

  1. 1

    Iteration 1: explore every flow

    Launch, search, generate, create variations, edit, dropdowns and gallery, all mapped. Search let users find an existing illustration, generate a new one with credits, or request one from the team.

  2. 2

    Iteration 2: cut the extras

    Fewer entry points and states, so a designer could go from opening the plugin to inserting an illustration in as few steps as possible.

  3. 3

    Iteration 3: two jobs, one clear path

    Home shows just the two main jobs. Sign-in happens only when someone generates, and editing offers Chat for quick changes in words or Precision to brush over the exact area to change.

06 · Measuring success

How I'd know the plugin is working

The plugin was a design sprint, so these are the signals I set out to track once it ships, not results.

  • Time to insert: from opening the plugin to an illustration on the canvas, in minutes instead of days
  • Iteration speed: regenerations and edits per illustration, instead of revision requests
  • Brand consistency: how often trained styles are chosen over generic ones
  • Reuse rate: saved illustrations used again, a sign of long-term value

07 · Key learning

Design for how people think, not just what they type.

Some users arrive with a picture in mind; others arrive with words. Giving each group its own front door (a prompt or a pack) did more for the product than any single screen.
