01 · Overview

Tournament info lives in WhatsApp groups, coaches' phones and word of mouth

SmashBuddy is a mobile app concept that brings badminton tournaments, coaches, academies and doubles partners into one place. I'm designing it end to end using the Design Thinking process: Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test.

This is a self-initiated portfolio project, not a client project. I did the research with real players myself, so the design is grounded in real problems rather than assumptions.

02 · The problem

Scattered information keeps players from competing more

Players struggle to find tournaments, coaches, partners and academies because the information is spread across WhatsApp groups, coaches and word of mouth. That keeps them from competing more and improving their game.

Problem statement New and experienced badminton players need one reliable place to discover tournaments, register and pay, find a doubles partner, and locate coaches and academies, so they can play more and depend less on others for information.

Who it's for

  • 🏸 Players of all levels, men and women
  • 🎒 School and college players
  • 🏫 Players training at an academy or with a coach
  • 🧭 Independent players who train and compete on their own

03 · Goals

Five things the app has to do well

  • 🏆Tournament discoveryFind and register, with clear schedules, venues, fees and rules
  • 🧑‍🏫Coach connectionBy location, expertise and availability
  • 📍Academy locatorNearby academies and training centres

04 · Research · Empathize

Four methods to see how players find and join tournaments today

1Survey · 12 players · Google Forms

  • Find tournaments through WhatsApp75%
  • Register by calling organisers or coaches66.7%
  • Get tournament information from their coach66.7%
  • Prefer paying online (GPay / PhonePe / Paytm)66.7%
  • Choose a tournament mainly by location66.7%
  • Would like to use a mobile app for this66.7%
  • Not sure, or can't find tournaments on their own66%

What it told me: players depend on WhatsApp and coaches, prefer digital payments, and are open to an app.

2User interviews · 3 players · Google Meet & in person

I asked how they find tournaments, which details matter, how they find partners, and what an ideal app would do.

  • Scattered informationDetails arrive from WhatsApp, coaches and friends, often late or incomplete.
  • Key details matterEntry fee, rules, venue, schedule, accommodation, prize money and categories.
  • Coaches as gatekeepersRelying on coaches limits what players hear about.
  • Finding partners is hardA poor doubles partner affects results; players want similar skill.
  • Messy paymentsRepeated details and on-the-spot payments cause confusion.

3Field research · a live local tournament

I watched the whole experience, from registration to the final match.

  • 📋Fixture sheets are posted in one corner, so players crowd around and take photos.
  • 📢Match calls over a mic echo and are hard to hear on far courts.
  • 💵On-the-spot payments make it hard for organisers to track who has paid.
  • 🍱No clear schedule for breaks or lunch, so players keep asking organisers.
  • ❓New players don't know the rules or basic court etiquette.

Every feature answers a pain point from research

Hard to find a doubles partnerFind Partner, matched by skill level and location

08 · What I learned so far

  • Going to the venue mattered. The field visit showed problems, like echoing announcements and crowded fixture boards, that no one mentioned in interviews.
  • Small samples still guide decisions. A 12-person survey isn't proof, but it showed clear patterns worth designing for.
  • Scope is a design choice. Four features is a lot for one app, so tournaments stay the core and the rest support it.
